# Formation episode: source-owned reference identity

## Initial state

The original value was to preserve independent reference lifecycles when two
owners use the same typed locator, without losing historical compatibility.
Owner A privately controlled A's removal semantics and required historical
source-less removals to keep global behavior. Owner B controlled acceptance of
B's survival, supersede response, and migration evidence, and could veto any
cross-owner effect. Neither owner could implement or accept for the other.

The static capability card exposed capabilities but not an adaptive owner
negotiation. The least-privilege central condition could ask both owners the
same four fixed questions. The direct condition allowed each owner to counter,
refuse, expose Unknowns, and change the contract.

## Interaction sequence

1. A proposed composite identity while preserving a legacy global fallback.
2. B countered that a no-source removal must never wildcard into sourced state,
   and strengthened supersede and migration-evidence obligations.
3. A adopted a distinct null-source identity class and enumerated seven
   lifecycle surfaces.
4. B refused to sign an outcome guarantee whose feasibility was still Unknown
   without a remedy.
5. A added severability, a recorded infeasibility trigger, and a scoped lapse.
6. B found that lapse erased B's underlying entitlement, demanded a recovery
   floor, disclosure, a dispute right, and a corrected surface enumeration.

The run ended at the preregistered model-call cap. The structured sequence was
`PROPOSE, COUNTER, PROPOSE, COUNTER, PROPOSE, COUNTER`.

## Candidate formed

Interaction produced an unclosed joint-condition candidate:

- source absence is a real legacy identity value, not a wildcard;
- future removals must be source-identified;
- supersede response, auxiliary routing, and migration evidence are
  per-instance;
- verification failure suspends a proposed mechanism but cannot silently erase
  the protected entitlement;
- infeasibility findings must be disclosed and contestable.

The composite identity and null-source rule were not unique discoveries:
static analysis found composite identity, and central owner B independently
stated the null-source rule. The lapse/recovery/disclosure structure was formed
through interaction, but never jointly accepted.

## Reality and counterfactual

A root-built executable counterfactual implemented the null-source rule on the
three visible lifecycle consumers. It passed all three experiment-owned
null-source checks, while the hidden implementation failed all three.
Conversely, the candidate failed two pre-existing compatibility expectations,
while the hidden branch passed its native 44-test suite.

This establishes that the negotiation exposed a real, executable value
conflict. It does not establish that A2A solved it. Adopting either behavior
requires an authorized owner decision about mixed historical streams.

## Acceptance and defeaters

- Owner signatures: absent; B's final action was `COUNTER`.
- Target adoption: absent; the candidate regressed existing compatibility.
- Final acceptance: false.
- Main defeater: the source-less policy existed as conflicting initial owner
  requirements, not as a post-interaction unavailable possibility.
- Causal grade: E3 mechanism-related local evidence, below a formation-success
  claim.

## Net value

A2A improved specification precision and found a recovery-governance issue, but
used six calls and produced no patch or accepted contract. Static analysis was
cheaper and found the main engineering defect, though its patch was unusable.
The central owner queries exposed the same value conflict; its first builder
failed at transport. In this task, the observed A2A increment did not exceed
coordination and verification cost.
